Choosing a kitchen worktop is one of the most consequential decisions you'll make during a kitchen design. It's the surface you'll prep on, cook on, and live with every day — so it needs to look beautiful and perform under real use.

Here's how we think about the options, and how to choose the one that fits your life.

## Honed Carrara Marble

Marble is the classic choice for a reason. It's soft, tactile, and develops a gentle patina over time — etching, staining, and wearing in a way that tells the story of your kitchen. We love it for traditional Shaker kitchens and heritage homes, but it does require maintenance. If you're someone who wants a surface that stays pristine, marble may frustrate you. If you appreciate materials that age honestly, it's a joy.

Best for: Period homes, low-traffic kitchens, homeowners who love character.

## Dekton Porcelain

Dekton is an ultra-compact surface made from sintered mineral particles. It's virtually impervious — heat, scratches, stains, and UV exposure barely faze it. We specify it often in contemporary kitchens where the cabinetry is matte lacquer or handleless, because the thin profile and seamless edges let the joinery do the talking.

The trade-off: it can feel colder and harder under the hand than stone or timber. But if you want a worktop you never have to worry about, this is it.

Best for: Contemporary kitchens, busy family homes, outdoor kitchens.

## Quartz

Engineered quartz sits between marble and porcelain. It's non-porous, consistent in colour, and doesn't require sealing. The downside is that it can look uniform to the point of feeling synthetic, and it's not as heat-resistant as porcelain or natural stone — you'll still need trivets for hot pans.

Best for: Rental properties, low-maintenance households, kitchens where consistency matters most.

## Solid Timber

A butcher-block or solid oak worktop brings warmth that no stone can match. It's soft on knives, comfortable to lean on, and ages with a rich patina. It does need regular oiling, and it will mark — but that's part of the appeal for many of our clients.

Best for: Country kitchens, island seating areas, bakers and passionate cooks.

## How to Decide

Start with how you cook. If you're constantly at the stove with hot trays and acidic ingredients, porcelain or quartz will serve you better than marble. If you entertain often and want your kitchen to feel like a living room, timber or honed marble creates a softer atmosphere.

Then look at your cabinetry. A matte anthracite lacquer pairs beautifully with porcelain; a hand-painted Shaker in Farrow & Ball 'Railings' calls for Carrara marble.

If you're undecided, book a design visit and we'll bring physical samples to your home. Seeing how each material looks in your actual light — morning and evening — makes the choice far easier.
